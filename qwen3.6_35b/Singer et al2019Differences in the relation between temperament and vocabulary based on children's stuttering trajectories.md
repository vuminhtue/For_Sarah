# 2. Study and Data Extraction

## 2.1 Bibliographic information
- **Title:** Differences in the relation between temperament and vocabulary based on children's stuttering trajectories
- **Authors:** Cara M. Singer, Tedra A. Walden, Robin M. Jones
- **Year:** NR (Not provided in supplied text)
- **Journal:** Journal of Communication Disorders
- **DOI/URL:** NR

## 2.2 Study number and analytic samples
- **Study Number:** Single longitudinal study (Wave 1–4 data analyzed).
- **Relevance:** Directly examines the joint association of vocabulary development and caregiver-reported temperament across three preschool stuttering trajectories (persisting, recovered, non-stuttering).

## 2.3 Study design
- **Design:** Longitudinal observational cohort with repeated measures.
- **Waves/Timing:** 4 assessment visits spanning 24–32 months (every 7–10 months).
- **Data Source:** Part of a larger longitudinal Vanderbilt University investigation on linguistic and emotional contributions to childhood stuttering.
- **Recruitment:** Advertisements in a parent magazine, referrals from Vanderbilt Bill Wilkerson Center/health professionals, and self-referrals in Middle Tennessee.

## 2.4 Sample size
- **Enrolled/Eligible:** 206 initially approached; 98 met inclusion criteria.
- **Final Analytic Sample:** 60 children (24 non-stuttering, 26 recovered, 10 persisting).
- **Exclusions:** Age outside range (3;0–4;11), non-compliance, speech/language ability mismatches, attrition (32% volitional loss), gender/age balancing requirements for the non-stuttering control group, and missing temperament data (n=1).
- **Location:** Sec 2.1; Fig 1

## 2.6 Measures

### Temperament Measures
- **Construct/Facets:** Effortful Control (emotional regulation), Negative Emotionality (negative affectivity)
- **Instrument:** Children's Behavior Questionnaire (CBQ; full 237-item and short 70-item forms)
- **Informant:** Parent/caregiver report
- **Items:** 47 (full) / 22 (short) for Effortful Control; 62 (full) / 23 (short) for Negative Emotionality
- **Response Scale:** 7-point Likert (1 = extremely untrue, 7 = extremely true)
- **Reliability:** Form correlations r = .872 for both scales (p. 4 of manuscript text)
- **Time Point:** Repeated at all 4 visits
- **Rothbart-based:** Yes (explicitly cited Rothbart et al., 2001; aligns with Rothbart's tripartite model)

### Language Measures
- **Construct:** Receptive vocabulary, Expressive vocabulary
- **Instrument:** Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test, 4th edition (PPVT-4); Expressive Vocabulary Test, 2nd edition (EVT-2)
- **Category:** Standardized norm-referenced tests (raw/standard scores)
- **Score Type:** Standard scores (higher = better relative to age)
- **Reliability:** PPVT-4 internal consistency α = .95; test-retest α = .92. EVT-2 test-retest α = .84.
- **Time Point:** Administered at all 4 visits
- **Location:** Sec 2.3.1; Table 2

### Comparison Type
Continuous language ability (standard scores) compared with continuous temperament scores, analyzed using repeated-measure linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts, stratified by talker group (persisting, recovered, non-stuttering) and adjusted for age, SES, gender, visit number, and time.

**Substantive Conclusion:** Higher negative emotionality is significantly associated with lower receptive vocabulary, but this relationship is moderated by stuttering trajectory. The negative association is robust in children who stutter (both persisting and recovered) and absent in typically fluent peers. No significant associations were found between temperament (effortful control or negative emotionality) and expressive vocabulary.

# 3. Additional Associations Relevant to Interpretation

- **Talker Group Main Effect on Receptive Vocabulary:** Significant difference across groups (d = 0.380, p = .004), with non-stuttering children scoring higher (M = 117.5) than recovered (M = 115.7) and persisting (M = 115.6) groups. (Sec 3.2.1; Table 3)
- **Covariate Effects on Receptive Vocabulary:** Gender (d = 0.571, p < .001), SES (d = 0.449, p = .005), and Age (d = 0.358, p = .030) significantly predicted receptive vocabulary. (Table 3)
- **Covariate Effects on Expressive Vocabulary:** Only Age was significant (d = 0.411, p = .012). Gender and SES were non-significant. (Table 3)

### Overall NIH quality judgment

**Overall rating: Fair**

### Strengths
- Clear longitudinal design with repeated measures of both exposure (temperament) and outcome (vocabulary).
- Well-established, reliable measures for both domains.
- Appropriate statistical modeling (linear mixed-effects) with control for major confounders (age, SES, gender).
- Transparent reporting of group compositions and exclusion pathways.

### Main risks of bias and limitations
- Substantial attrition (32%) exceeds the NIH threshold of 20%, potentially introducing bias if dropout correlates with temperament or vocabulary trajectory.
- Lack of reported power analysis or sample-size justification.
- No documentation of assessor blinding, which may introduce expectancy effects in standardized testing.
- Small subsample size for the persisting group (n=10), limiting power to detect group-specific moderation effects.

---

# 5. Final Review Decision

**Protocol decision: Include**

The study aligns with the review's focus on child temperament and language ability in typically developing preschoolers. It utilizes a Rothbart-based framework, reports core dimensions, and provides direct statistical associations between temperament and vocabulary. Scientific relevance is high for understanding how emotional reactivity interacts with stuttering trajectory to influence receptive language development.
